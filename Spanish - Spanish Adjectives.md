# Spanish Adjectives

## 📌 Grammar Rule

Adjectives describe or modify nouns. The source covers their **types, gender and number agreement, position, comparison, limiting modifiers, adjective clauses, and adjective formation**.

Descriptive adjectives generally agree with the noun in **gender and number**.

## 🧩 Structure

### Basic agreement

`noun + adjective`

or, depending on the adjective and meaning:

`adjective + noun`

### Gender

Typical pattern:

`-o -> -a`

Example: `alto -> alta`

### Number

- Singular stays singular.
- After a vowel, plural commonly adds `-s`.
- After most consonants, plural commonly adds `-es`.

### Comparison

`más + adjective + que`

`menos + adjective + que`

### Relative superlative

`definite article + más/menos + adjective`

### Adjective placement

Most descriptive adjectives follow the noun:

- `Es una casa grande.` — It is a big house.
- `Tengo un perro feliz.` — I have a happy dog.

Quantifying adjectives generally precede the noun:

- `Hay muchos libros.` — There are many books.
- `Tiene pocos amigos.` — He/She has few friends.

Possessive adjectives precede the noun:

- `Este es mi coche.` — This is my car.
- `Vendieron nuestra casa.` — They sold our house.

### Position can change meaning or nuance

- `Es mi antiguo amigo.` — He is my former friend.
- `Es una casa antigua.` — It is an old house.
- `Fue un gran hombre.` — He was a great man.
- `Es un hombre grande.` — He is a large man.

The source notes that usage can vary by region and context, and common expressions and intended emphasis matter.

### Comparatives

- `Juan es más alto que Pedro.` — Juan is taller than Pedro.
- `Este coche es menos rápido que aquel.` — This car is less fast than that one.

### Irregular comparatives

| Base adjective | Irregular comparative | Meaning in source |
|---|---|---|
| `bueno` | `mejor` | better |
| `malo` | `peor` | worse |
| `grande` | `mayor` | older / greater |
| `pequeño` | `menor` | younger / lesser |

The source adds:

- `mayor` is especially associated with age, rank, or importance; `más grande` is usual for physical size.
- `menor` is especially associated with age, rank, or importance; `más pequeño` is usual for physical size.
- `viejo` can correspond to `mayor` when referring to an older person, but `más viejo` is also used; `mayor` is not a general replacement for “old.”
- `joven` can correspond to `menor` in contexts referring to a minor or a younger person in a comparison, but `más joven` is the ordinary comparative for age.
- For “less good,” the source gives the regular expression `menos bueno`.

### Descriptive vs limiting modifiers

Descriptive adjectives characterize a noun:

- `Es un libro interesante.` — It is an interesting book.
- `Vivo en una casa grande.` — I live in a big house.
- `Oigo perros ruidosos.` — I hear noisy dogs.
- `Compré flores bonitas.` — I bought beautiful flowers.

Limiting modifiers specify or restrict a noun. The source includes articles, demonstratives, possessives, interrogatives, and quantifiers:

- `Compré el libro.` — I bought the book.
- `Quiero este libro.` — I want this book.
- `He perdido mi libro.` — I have lost my book.
- `¿Qué libro quieres?` — Which book do you want?
- `Leí algunos libros.` — I read some books.

> **Key distinction:** descriptive adjectives characterize the noun; limiting modifiers identify, quantify, or establish its relation.

### Adjective clauses / relative clauses

An adjective clause is a subordinate clause that describes or gives more information about a noun.

Common relative words/forms shown in the source:

- `que` — that, which, who
- `quien`, `quienes` — who, whom (normally people)
- `cuyo`, `cuya`, `cuyos`, `cuyas` — whose
- `el cual`, `la cual`, `los cuales`, `las cuales` — which, who
- `el que`, `la que`, `los que`, `las que` — that, which, who
- `lo que`, `lo cual` — what, which, referring to an idea or unspecified thing

Defining clause:

- `El libro que compré ayer es muy interesante.` — The book that I bought yesterday is very interesting.

Non-defining clause:

- `Mi hermano, quien es médico, vive en Madrid.` — My brother, who is a doctor, lives in Madrid.

### Adjectives formed from other words

The source says these patterns are productive in some words but not completely predictable, so many forms must be learned individually.

From nouns, examples include:

- `rocoso` from `roca`
- `personal` from `persona`
- `histórico` from `historia`
- `divino` from `divinidad`

From verbs, examples include:

- `amante` from `amar`
- `creyente` from `creer`
- `visible` from `ver`
- `amable` from `amar`

| Pattern | Example |
|---|---|
| noun + `-al` | `personal`, from `persona` |
| noun + `-oso` | `nervioso`, from `nervio` |
| verb + `-ente` | `creyente`, from `creer` |
| verb + `-able` | `amable`, from `amar` |

### Special agreement patterns

- Adjectives ending in `-or`, `-án`, `-ón`, or `-ín` commonly add `-a` in the feminine: `un hombre hablador` / `una mujer habladora`.
- Some adjectives have one form for both genders, especially those ending in `-e` or many consonants: `un estudiante inteligente` / `una estudiante inteligente`.
- Nationality adjectives ending in a consonant commonly add `-a` in the feminine: `un hombre español` / `una mujer española`.

## ⚠️ Important Notes

- The source explicitly says that `-o -> -a` is a **typical pattern, not a universal rule**.
- Adjectives ending in `-e` or a consonant often have the same masculine and feminine form, but still agree in number.
- Mixed-gender groups traditionally take masculine plural agreement in the source's examples.
- Adjective placement depends on the adjective, meaning, emphasis, and context.
- Some adjectives can change meaning or nuance according to position.
- Some comparative forms are irregular and should not be confused with ordinary translations.
- In non-defining adjective clauses, commas are important.
